Architectural field notes · Finding the tools

A Game I Could Build With

What if a streamer’s community could build its own history through play? Not just finish dungeons, but change the world they returned to. The Living World is that vision: the same starting content, different communities, different futures. Finding the tools to build it changed the direction of the game.

01The ambition
More than another dungeon run

A world that belonged to its community

Streamers already set subscriber goals to unlock extra time or a weekend stream. What if turning a goblin camp together unlocked that weekend stream? I wanted a business that gave those customers something worth building around: a world their players could change. Failed hostage rescues could create future enemies. Another Discord server could play the same starting content and end up with a different history.

02The constraint
Working was not the same as useful

I had an application, not the tools

Moving Encounter into the toolkit had made its shape easier to see. I knew it was still an application. The crisis came from trying to see how the Living World could come to life inside it. Movement, knowledge, combat and time were tangled together. The prototype had taught us valuable things, but I couldn’t pick up its pieces and build the situations I imagined. A tool builder without tools: that was what needed to change.

03The tools
Small enough to use together

Knowledge and time became pieces

Two of the simplest packages made the biggest difference. Intel held what an observer knew, whether that observer was a player or a monster. Clock handled advancement through turns or world time. Neither needed to understand the whole encounter. The composition could read what someone had learned and decide whether they belonged in a fight. Knowledge could matter without becoming another rule hidden inside the clock.

04The proof
One fight, not everybody’s fight

The straggler could keep moving

Divinity: Original Sin 2 gave me the picture of a fight forming locally. Our composition made landed intel the input, rather than a simple proximity rule. In the split-party test, Alice and a goblin entered combat while the others stayed on the world clock. Carl could still move. The fight hadn’t stopped the world. That was a small example with an enormous implication.

What if sneaking or an illusion kept the relevant intel from landing? Those weren’t finished features. They were possibilities I could now see a way to build.

05The horizon
The beginning of building seriously

Something to build with

Seeing those pieces compose was when the game began to feel serious to me. The play/* tools, and later the world/* examples, made the ambition tangible. Not a finished living world, but scaffolding that could support one. A community’s shared goal, a rescue that changed tomorrow’s enemies, a camp that believed the wrong person: these were becoming things I could reason about building.

I collected the larger vision and its executable examples in The Living World →.

I didn’t have the whole world yet.
I was beginning to have the tools to build it.

Evidence trail

The Living World ambition and the feeling of being a tool builder without tools are my recollections. The August 8 reset record names the application/toolbox distinction. The old application kept running while the new pieces were built.

The August 15 trigger composition reads intel’s first-contact reports. The split-party test asserts the fight and continuing free roam; its later straggler entry uses an explicit transfer. That version used symmetric sight, supported one fight bubble, and did not implement the stealth or illusion possibilities described here.

The August 30 camp example and August 31 world graduation are a later, parallel expression of composition, not a claim that Encounter was extracted from those packages. The written Living World record follows the reset; it does not date when I first imagined it.
