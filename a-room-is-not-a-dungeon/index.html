Pick up the last thread

The driver was already there

A Flurry Strike did not load its own combatants, create their bus, or decide what the client should receive. The encounter orchestrator assembled that work and called the toolkit’s rules. I already knew the API was doing more than I wanted. This was deliberately where I was learning what a simpler boundary would need to carry.

Owning a change is not the same as completing an interaction.
02Alongside the rules
The tool underneath the room

Everything could start at zero

tools/spatial gave us rooms, coordinates, entity placement, and movement checks. It handled space, not D&D rules. My buddy built that foundation. He wasn’t a programmer by trade, but he had a knack for seeing how things fit and solving problems. In the first room, at the dungeon origin, local and dungeon positions coincided. I expected opening the door and continuing into the next room to be the easy part.

One room had hidden an assumption. The next room could not.
03Feb → March 2026
Make the relationship explicit

The room’s contents have to arrive with it

The early fix made that relationship explicit. The API calculated and stored room origins; the client accumulated revealed rooms. March’s follow-through exposed a finer mismatch: a room’s walls could be shifted into dungeon space while its creatures stayed local. The conversion had to carry both. Monster IDs also had to distinguish one room’s inhabitants from another’s.

A shared coordinate frame has to include what lives inside it.
04Still in the prototype
The next limit is behind the picture

Revealing the room was not enough

Showing the next room did not complete the handoff. OpenDoor could announce it and add its monsters to the encounter, yet leave their spatial placements out of the saved RoomData used by movement and perception. The client could be shown a larger dungeon while those server routines still read the old room.

The next room could appear on the screen.
The driver still needed a world that included it.
