Layer 7 · The action system

An Action Could Grant the Next Step

The door had opened, but the monsters would not cross. I hadn’t solved that. As I remember it, we turned back to the action system. The important change was a new kind of building block: Actions a Character could have, gain, and use. Taking one could grant the next step, instead of carrying out the whole interaction at once.

01Back to the actions
Counting was not the whole model

A counter was not an Action

We could count what remained in a turn: an action, a bonus action, a reaction. But “one action left” did not describe the things a Character could actually do, or the new opportunities a choice might create. That behavior still had to be assembled around the counters.

A budget alone cannot describe a grantable next step.
02New building block
This was the shift

Actions became things a Character could gain

We made Actions first-class, grantable things. Taking an action could create an opportunity for another, rather than completing the whole interaction itself. Attack grants the capacity to make a Strike; choosing that Strike is a separate step. Characters could have ordinary base actions and gain further possibilities through the same action system.

An action could change what you were able to do next.
03March 21, 2026
Give the new model a coherent home

The economy supported the action system

The economy tracked what could be spent, what had been granted, and what remained. The action system gave those facts meaning. Character could perform the economy change and return the resulting choices together. That let the API delegate instead of rebuilding the action rules alongside the toolkit. The cleanup followed from the model.

Ask the owner what the change makes possible.
04A bounded win
A new possibility, not just a smaller count

A spent action could leave a Strike waiting

The player could spend the turn action and still have a Strike waiting. That was the point: the first choice had granted a separate next step. Character could retain that opportunity, let it be used, and report what remained. The follow-up no longer had to be hidden inside one larger operation.

Evidence trail

The unfinished crossing and the remembered shift in attention come from Kirk’s reconstruction. He remembers the door opening but monsters not crossing; the exact sequence and cause are not pinned. This chapter does not claim the doorway was fixed, or that Actions were first invented afterward.

The January Action split makes Actions first-class and grantable. The budget/capacity change adds granted attacks and movement to the earlier resource-only ledger. The ordinary-action setup supplies combat abilities such as Attack and permanent Strike/Move Actions. Attack grants capacity, not a newly constructed Strike object on each use. Flurry and Bless were future design cases Kirk measured the shape against, not proof of a level-one Flurry playthrough.

The concrete refinement is Character-owned economy and availability, integrated March 21. The turn/activation tests cover starting the turn, granting a strike and refusing another standard-action ability; the strike tests cover consuming capacity and refusing a strike without it. The ordinary-turn diagram illustrates those semantics, not a captured game session.

API f4c6284 deletes its availability builders and delegates these economy operations. The March 21 integration pins the released rulebook module rather than the intermediate local replacement. The API still drives attack resolution and movement, and persists several kinds of state. Character’s Move branch at this point checks availability; it does not implement spatial movement or debit its distance.

The chapter follows the developing Action model from its January introduction through March’s ownership refinement; it does not date both changes to one moment. The win is a grantable next step, not a complete encounter rewrite or a claim of one literal economy caller across every repository.
