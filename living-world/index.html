The Living World

How the game becomes more than a dungeon clearer: every action becomes a fact, facts fold into each witness's present, predicates watch the folds, and the world moves — for one rogue, for one camp, for a whole Discord server at once. Everything on this page is merged and under test.

1 · The life of one act

Rook tries to slip past the bandit camp. No prerequisite is checked — anyone may attempt anything; the sheet only tilts the dice. The act enters through the composer's one write door, the rulebook judges it, and what comes out is not "success" — it is a fact with an audience.

2 · Knowledge is the audience of facts

Nothing stores "what the camp believes." There is no belief database anywhere. Every observer — a player, a camp, one suspicious lieutenant — derives its present by folding only the facts it witnessed. The changeling play works because the kill fact never reached the camp; the blown disguise works because the reveal reached exactly one pair of eyes.

The ruling that started it: “our events are player-detected.” Generalize player to entity and stealth, disguise, false belief, and dramatic irony all fall out of one mechanism. The streamer's camera sees everything; each character sees only their own feed.

3 · Every way in is a check, and no way is gated

Content is the goal, not the path. The camp offers many ways in; none has a prerequisite. The barbarian in a goblin costume is a legitimate play — expertise moves the odds, the dice decide, and each route differs in what the camp witnesses and therefore what fight, if any, you get.

4 · Populations, not quest trees

Quests come off the board: a claim mints an instance, and your party's hostage is your hostage — three companies can fail and succeed in parallel without ever colliding. The world changes by folding the whole population, and when the fold crosses a declared shape, the next chapter opens itself. Nobody authors a tree; the community's aggregate performance walks the world through states no one scripted.

5 · One needle for the whole server

The Discord server is the world — one journal, every party writing into it. A world goal is a fold over all of it with a deadline: the streamer declares “pacify the region before the weekend,” and three companies push one needle by entirely different methods, because folds don't ask how.

6 · The DM seat and its three tenants

The seat is a client, never the core. It speaks the same verbs as everyone else, so it can only do what the verbs allow — which bounds a hallucinating model and a chaos-minded streamer with the same law. Three tenants, ascending intelligence, one protocol:

  • Dice tables — ship with v1. When the world needs a small decision nobody authored (a rescued hostage's fate), roll on a table.
  • The streamer — the paying human. Declares world goals, sets deadlines and population dials; our tools advise the numbers, the seat decides them.
  • The local model — arrives last, onto a road-tested seat: naming hostages first, parsing “I search the desk” later, improvising over authored content in time.

7 · The machine's parts

Everything above runs on four kernel packages, a small composer, and content that is just declarations. The kernel never learns a d20 exists; the rulebook enters through one injected seam. All of it lives in examples/world — its temp home — until the seams are walked and the packages graduate.

8 · Where this meets the game you already have

  • Discord server
    = the world: one guild journal, seats capped and concurrent — the tenant, the world, and the billing unit are one noun.
  • the town
    a thin lobby over Discord's own voice and presence: the quest board where claims happen, slowly filling with the people the community actually rescued.
  • dungeon runs
    instanced as today; outcomes flow back as facts. The run-ending work was quest v0 all along.
  • the combat log
    the journal is the log that never gets thrown away — same law, bigger clock. Detection beats already go to the players who earned them.
  • dungeon builder
    scenario packages are its checklist: the config a camp requires today is the form a streamer fills tomorrow, and constructor refusals are its inline guidance.
  • monster behavior
    Billy's lane reads the same folds — “who leads my camp, how do I feel about these players” — and the DM seat is the monster seat, grown up.
  • NPCs
    FadedPez's feature: actors on the same bus, first voices for the model to wear.
