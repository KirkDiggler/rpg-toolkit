One World, Different Views

Moving Encounter into the toolkit hadn’t made it a toolbox. But there was something inside it I wanted to see working: one shared encounter, with each player receiving their own view of what happened.

01The May prototype
I needed proof

The events belonged on the screen

The characters were rough and the controls looked like development tools. That was fine. I’m a systems builder. I wanted the game and the events where I could inspect them together. Seeing an attack work mattered. Seeing what each player received mattered more.

02The test I remember
One door, two perspectives

Fifteen feet made a difference

The first test I remember was opening a door with a second player fifteen feet behind. The second player’s view didn’t reach as far into the room. It worked the first time. I made the fog of war stronger so I could see the line-of-sight angles more clearly.

03Inside the stream
Not just a different camera

The account itself was different

It wasn’t enough to choose who received an event. The system also had to choose which parts they received. A movement could be fully visible to one player and only briefly visible to another. The server sent each player their portion, not the whole path for the browser to hide.

That exercised more than visibility. The player’s perspective had to survive the event structure, routing, translation and client state. The event panels let me inspect the result beside the game.

04An early beginning
Out of sight is not never seen

The last place I saw it

There were beginnings of remembering, too. When an entity disappeared from view, the client could keep a ghost at the last position that player had seen. Not its actual new position. That distinction mattered: the world could continue changing without silently updating what the player knew.

05The horizon
More than making the prototype work

How far could we take this?

Each player getting their own view changed what I could imagine building. Split the party. Let something slip out of sight. Make the place someone last saw it matter. Those weren’t all finished features. They were reasons to keep going.

The prototype had proved something worth carrying forward. It hadn’t yet proved that the application around it could support everything that possibility opened up.

One world didn’t have to mean one shared view.
How much game could we build from that difference?
